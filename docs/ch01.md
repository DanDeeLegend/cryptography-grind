## 1.1 Break a shift/substitution cipher

**Idea.** A substitution cipher renames letters but cannot rename the *habits* of a language: `e` is common, `q` is rare, `th`
is a frequent pair. Those habits survive encryption, so the ciphertext leaks its key.

**Caesar (26 keys).** For each shift, compare the ciphertext's letter counts to English letter frequencies with a chi-squared
statistic and keep the best shift. It recovered the key from **120 characters** in the demo (a few dozen are usually enough).

**General substitution (26! ≈ 2^88 keys).** Too many to try, so it is a search problem: start from a guess that maps the
most common ciphertext letter to `e`, the next to `t`, and so on, then repeatedly swap two letters of the key and keep the swap
whenever the decrypted text looks more English. "Looks English" is a score from a letter-triple model (trigram, blended with
bigram and single-letter statistics because the small training corpus makes trigrams sparse).

**How many characters were needed?** Measured on a passage the model was *not* trained on, 5 random keys per length,
"success" = at least 95% of letters correct:

| Ciphertext length | Mean accuracy | Runs with ≥ 95% correct |
|---:|---:|---:|
| 50 | 10.8% | 0% |
| 100 | 63.4% | 0% |
| 150 | 50.5% | 0% |
| 200 | 65.6% | 0% |
| 300 | 89.0% | 40% |
| **400** | **100%** | **100%** |
| 600 | 100% | 100% |

**Takeaway.** About **400 characters** (roughly 70 words) break a monoalphabetic cipher reliably, with a small training
corpus. A larger corpus would lower that. This is why Kerckhoffs's principle matters: the keyspace (2^88) was never the
real security, the *structure* of the cipher was the weakness.

## 1.4 Brute-force feasibility calculator

Average time to find a key is half the key space: 2^(bits-1) guesses. The attacker speeds are **illustrative round numbers**
(not measurements); edit `ATTACKERS` to model a real adversary.

| Key bits | 1 GPU (1e9 keys/s) | Cloud cluster (1e12/s) | Nation-state (1e18/s) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 40 | 9.2 minutes | <1 second | <1 second |
| 56 | 1.1 years | 10.0 hours | <1 second |
| 64 | 292 years | 106.8 days | 9.2 seconds |
| 80 | 1.9e+07 years | 19,154 years | 7.0 days |
| 128 | 3.9e+11 × age of universe | 3.9e+08 × | 3.9e+02 × |
| 256 | 1.3e+50 × | 1.3e+47 × | 1.3e+41 × |

**Interpretation.** Each extra bit doubles the work, so the table is a lesson in exponentials. 40-bit keys are trivial for
anyone; 56-bit (DES) fell to a cluster-class attacker long ago; 64-bit is within reach of a well-funded adversary; **80-bit
is the first size that survives a cloud cluster, and only 128-bit and above hold against a nation-state**, even if they
gain a factor of a billion. Past 128 bits brute force is never the weak point: attackers go after implementations,
key management and people instead. (Quantum search would roughly halve the *effective* bits, which is why 256-bit keys are
recommended for long-term secrets.)
